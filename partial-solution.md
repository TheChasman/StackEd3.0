## HOOK

In 2002, the oath taken by every police officer in England and Wales was quietly rewritten. The old wording asked constables to serve "without favour or affection, malice or ill will." The new version replaced those four plain negatives with something rather more aspirational: "with fairness, integrity, diligence and impartiality, upholding fundamental human rights and according equal respect to all people."

**[SCAFFOLD: Opening with the oath change establishes the timeline and gives the reader a concrete fact to anchor the philosophical argument that follows. If this feels too procedural, the Alt Hook below leads with the emotional hook instead.]**

**[ALT HOOK: We all know what partiality looks like — it shouts. What we can't see is impartiality. It doesn't make a sound.]**

Notice what happened there. The old oath described what an officer should *not* do. The new one describes what they *should* be. That's a bigger ask than it sounds.

## CONTEXT

Partiality is easy to spot. Two-Tier Policing, as the tabloids call it. Protests policed differently depending on who's protesting and what they're carrying. Social media is awash with side-by-side footage that appears to show exactly that — one crowd kettleed and batoned, another met with community liaison officers and a gentle word.

Whether that footage tells the whole truth is almost beside the point. The perception of partiality does its damage regardless. Trust, once seen through the lens of bias, doesn't recover easily.

But impartiality? That's the ghost. You can't photograph it. You can't clip it and share it. When a police officer treats two people exactly the same, there's no story. No headline. No viral thread. Impartiality is invisible by its nature. It's the dog that doesn't bark.

## Bad Seed

The problem runs deeper than policing. Think about the cases where impartiality *appeared* to arrive — and how it landed. Harvey Weinstein. Jeffrey Epstein. Men whose social standing had, for decades, seemed to place them beyond the reach of ordinary justice. When the law finally caught up, the public reaction wasn't relief that impartiality had prevailed. It was fury that it had taken so long.

**[SCAFFOLD: Using Weinstein and Epstein from the SPARK anchors the argument in the concrete, but pivots from the expected reaction (satisfaction) to the unexpected one (anger at delay). This sets up the TURN later.]**

That's the paradox. Impartiality arriving late looks exactly like partiality in reverse. The people who'd been protected are now being punished — and some will see that as the system overcorrecting, not the system working.

## Lean-Burn

The 2002 oath change was well-intentioned. You can see what the draftsmen wanted: to move from a negative constraint (don't be biased) to a positive aspiration (be fair). But aspirations are harder to enforce than prohibitions. "Without favour" is a test you can fail. "With impartiality" is a quality you must possess — and who measures that?

**[IMAGE: Scales of justice, Pixabay search: "scales justice silhouette" — keeping it minimal, not the gavel-and-wig cliché]**

The old oath had the virtue of clarity. Four things to avoid. Easy to remember, easy to test against. The new oath wraps fairness in a ribbon of human rights language that sounds magnificent in a seminar and means very little on a rainy Friday night in Croydon.

## Printing Money

Here's the thing nobody wants to say out loud: impartiality might not be something humans can reliably *do*. We can avoid obvious bias. We can build systems with checks and appeals. We can train people to recognise their own prejudices. But the state of being truly impartial — utterly free from favour — is a philosophical ideal, not a practical one.

The old oath was honest about that. It didn't ask you to be impartial. It asked you to resist the things that make you partial. There's a difference, and it matters.

## TURN

So perhaps the real question isn't "what does impartiality look like?" but "what does partiality look like when we're honest about it?" The 2002 oath assumed we could aim for the positive and the negative would take care of itself. Twenty-odd years on, public trust in policing is lower than it's been in living memory. The aspiration may have been the wrong target.

**[SCAFFOLD: The TURN reframes the argument — we've been chasing the wrong thing. Not "how do we become impartial" but "how do we stay honest about our partiality." This is the hinge the piece has been building toward.]**

The old wording — without favour, without malice — was modest and negative. It didn't promise fairness. It promised the *absence of unfairness*. That's a smaller claim. But it's a claim you can keep.

## LANDING

We've spent two decades demanding that institutions embody virtues they can't consistently demonstrate. The result isn't more fairness — it's more disappointment when the inevitable gap between aspiration and reality becomes visible. Maybe the old draftsmen, with their four plain negatives, understood something we've forgotten: that the best way to protect fairness isn't to promise it, but to chip away at the things that undermine it.

That's my two penn'orth. Live well, stay safe, and read the small print.
